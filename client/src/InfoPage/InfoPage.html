Core Concepts in Quantum Computation

Quantum computation is a computational paradigm based on the principles of quantum mechanics. Classical bits are replaced by quantum bits (qubits) that can exist in multiple states simultaneously. Quantum operations are unitary and reversible, and the circuit model serves as a universal framework for quantum algorithms.

Key distinctions from classical computation:

  • Reversibility — Most quantum operations preserve information.
  • Parallelism — Superposition enables evaluation of multiple possibilities simultaneously.
  • Interference — Amplitudes can constructively or destructively combine.

Quantum Bits (Qubits)

A single qubit is described by a normalized vector in a two-dimensional complex Hilbert space:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩  where  |α|² + |β|² = 1

Multi-qubit systems scale exponentially: an n-qubit register lives in a 2ⁿ-dimensional Hilbert space.

Key Properties

  • Superposition — A qubit can represent both 0 and 1 simultaneously.
  • Measurement — Collapses the state probabilistically to |0⟩ or |1⟩.
  • Entanglement — Creates non-classical correlations between multiple qubits.

Quantum Gates and Circuits

Quantum circuits consist of unitary gates applied sequentially to qubits, followed by measurement. Gates are represented by unitary matrices that evolve the quantum state while preserving its norm.

Multi-Qubit Gates

Multi-qubit gates operate on two or more qubits and are essential for creating entanglement — the key non-classical resource in quantum computing.

3. Toffoli Gate (CCNOT)

Three-qubit gate — flips target only if both controls are |1⟩.

Note: This is an 8×8 matrix. It is universal for classical reversible computing and is heavily used in Shor’s algorithm for modular arithmetic.

Universality

Any quantum operation can be approximated using the universal gate set:

{ H, T, CNOT }

Quantum compilers automatically decompose complex algorithms into these native gates.

Fundamental Quantum Phenomena in Circuits

  • Superposition — Parallel evaluation of multiple inputs
  • Entanglement — Non-local correlations (e.g., Bell pair)
  • Interference — Constructive amplification of correct outcomes
  • No-Cloning Theorem — Prevents perfect copying of unknown quantum states (ignored in simulation)

Measurement and Readout

Measurement projects the quantum state onto classical bits. Circuits are executed multiple times (“shots”) to obtain probability distributions. In practice, this yields statistical results rather than deterministic outcomes.

Notable Quantum Algorithms

Each algorithm below can be visualized and simulated in the Interactive Lab.

1. Deutsch-Jozsa Algorithm

Determines whether a function f:{0,1}ⁿ → {0,1} is constant or balanced with a single query. Circuit: Hadamard layers + oracle + final Hadamards. Example (n=1): Distinguishes constant vs. balanced functions in one evaluation.

2. Grover’s Algorithm

Quadratic speedup for unstructured search over N = 2ⁿ items. Circuit: Oracle + diffusion operator repeated ≈ π/4√N times. Example (2 qubits): Searches a 4-element database with high probability after 1 iteration.

3. Shor’s Algorithm

Factors large integers in polynomial time. Core: Quantum period-finding via Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT). Example: Factoring 15 using 4–6 qubits.

4. Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE)

Estimates eigenvalues of unitary operators. Circuit: Controlled-U operations + inverse QFT. Foundational for quantum chemistry.

5. Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)

Hybrid algorithm for molecular ground-state energies on NISQ devices. Uses parameterized ansatz circuits optimized classically.

6. Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)

Solves combinatorial optimization problems (e.g., Max-Cut) with alternating problem and mixer layers.

Applications and Practical Considerations

Quantum circuits enable breakthroughs in cryptography, optimization, machine learning, quantum simulation, and finance. Current NISQ devices face noise and decoherence; error correction (e.g., surface code) is an active research area.
